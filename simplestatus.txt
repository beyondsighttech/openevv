3 Sep 2026 - HINDI LETTERS NOW SPEAK. This is the one that matters.
--------------------------------------------------------------------
A Devanagari letter now reaches a rule that says what it sounds like.
Before today the letters arrived and every word came out as the same
silence; that is fixed.

Proof - three words, three different lengths, three different hashes:
  namaste  49,643 samples  hash 8eae9806
  raam     27,797 samples  hash f7b80df2
  kamal    46,519 samples  hash a2d84e80

Before today all three were 18,557 samples under the one hash 47a8f230.
Same word, three different sounds, is the whole point.

And the vowel signs work. The same consonant with different signs comes
out at different lengths, which is what proves the sign is being read:
  क   (ka)   26,939 samples
  का  (kaa)  27,687 samples
  कि  (ki)   27,027 samples
  को  (ko)   27,467 samples

English did not move. The fox sentence still hashes f4de90c6, exactly
what it hashed before any of this.

WHY IT WAS BROKEN, and it was not the rules.
It was one line in a tool of mine. When Hindi took over 59 unused slots
in the alphabet, that tool also renamed each one - slot 157 became
"dka" instead of holding the character it held. Turns out a slot's name
is not a label. The engine reads an incoming byte, turns it into a
one-character string, and compares it against the names one by one
until it finds a match. THAT COMPARISON IS the byte-to-letter lookup -
there is no other table. So a slot named "dka" matched no byte at all.
Every Devanagari character was read, matched against nothing, and
thrown away. Hence identical silence for every word.

Fixed by putting the original names back and leaving the records alone,
which were always the correct half of the job. Added a "mend" command
so this is undoable rather than a hand edit.

Then the rules themselves - 420 of them, written by two tools:
  - tools/hien-thunks.py writes the 833 small wrappers the engine needs,
    plus the 330 consonant-with-vowel-sign combinations, generated from
    the basic sounds rather than typed out.
  - tools/hien-arms.py splices the 420 tests into the rule that speaks.

Two mistakes worth recording because each cost a day:
  - Put the new rules just ABOVE the right label instead of just below
    it. The engine loops back to that label, so anything above it ran
    once and was skipped forever after. Exactly one letter of six spoke.
  - Wrote the alphabet position wrong: used the byte a character arrives
    as, not the slot number the engine actually compares. That fails
    silently - every test just misses.

STILL NOT DONE, and this is audible:
  - राम comes out as "raama", not "raam". Hindi drops the built-in "a"
    at the end of a word and this does not yet. Needs a way to check
    for a word boundary without consuming it; every rule here consumes
    what it matches.
  - Eighteen letters still say a near neighbour: the aspirates (ख as क)
    and the whole retroflex series (ट as त). Those sounds do not exist
    in the phoneme set yet. Separate job, tools/lang-phonemes.py.
  - Nobody has listened to it. It makes distinct sounds per letter,
    which is measurable; whether it sounds like Hindi is not something
    a hash can tell you.


29 Aug 2026 - DEVANAGARI NOW ARRIVES PROPERLY. Half the gibberish fixed.
-------------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the real fix for yesterday's problem, and it works.

Before: नमस्ते (6 letters) arrived as 18 separate junk bytes, because
the engine had no idea Devanagari existed. It read out all 18.
After: it arrives as 6 letters.

Proof - the same word, before and after, in one run:
  नमस्ते  through Hindi    18,557 samples
  नमस्ते  through English  94,974 samples   (English still has no table)
That is one fifth the length. The 18 junk characters are gone.

How: Hindi's alphabet now has all 59 Devanagari characters in it -
33 consonants, 11 vowels, 10 vowel signs, 4 signs, and the danda.
Each has a byte, and a table says U+0928 arrives as that byte.

Where the slots came from: Hindi's alphabet was copied from English
and had 108 slots holding accented Latin letters that Hindi will
never use. Those were taken. Plain letters, digits and punctuation
were left alone, because Hindi text contains English words and
numbers.

BUT IT IS NOT FIXED YET, and I want to be straight about this.
The letters arrive, and no rule knows what any of them SOUNDS like.
So all three test words come out as the same 37,158 bytes - the same
default noise. Shorter than before, but not yet Hindi.

  namaste  18,557 samples  hash 47a8f230
  raam     18,557 samples  hash 47a8f230   <- same
  kamal    18,557 samples  hash 47a8f230   <- same

Made a checker for exactly this, test/hien-arrives.py. It says in one
line where the work stands. Right now it says: letters arrive and say
nothing of their own.

Also: test/hien-bless.sh now REFUSES to record hashes when different
words hash the same. It nearly recorded this silence as the correct
answer, which would have made the test permanently meaningless.

Two other things had to be built to get here:
  - Hindi's character records were English's 3-byte shape; the tool
    needs 5 (it wants a sound per letter). Widened them. Getting one
    byte of that wrong made all three hashes move - caught it, the
    accent byte has to be 1 not 0.
  - the tools could not tell "byte 0x80 is a real Hindi letter" from
    "byte 0x80 is nothing", because renaming a slot loses its byte.
    Added a small file that remembers.

28 Aug 2026 - Found out WHY Hindi sounds like gibberish
-------------------------------------------------------
You were right that it speaks nonsense. I measured the cause.

The word नमस्ते is 6 letters, but in the computer it is 18 bytes.
The engine reads ONE BYTE AT A TIME. It has no idea that 3 bytes
belong together as one letter. So it sees 18 pieces of junk and
reads out all 18.

Proof:
  नमस्ते through Hindi        = 94,974 samples (8.6 seconds)
  same bytes through English  = 94,974 samples (exactly the same)
  "namaste" in plain letters  = 11,484 samples (1 second, sounds fine)

Both languages take the same time, which proves neither understands
Devanagari - they are both just spelling out garbage.

Good news in that last line: typing "namaste" in normal letters
already speaks properly. So the engine's mouth works. What is
missing is only the part that turns Devanagari into sounds.

DECISION: you chose to do this properly, not with a shortcut. And
later: you said real Devanagari, not Hindi standing on another
language. So the alphabet was widened in place rather than reseeding
Hindi from Italian.

29 Aug 2026 - the letter rules exist. They are not wired in yet.
-----------------------------------------------------------------
Written this session:
  - 81 sound-strings (every sound a Hindi letter can say). Each
    consonant needs two: one bare, one with the "a" it carries.
  - 48 letter rules, one per Devanagari character. They compile to
    real machine code.
  - a generator, tools/hien-letters.py, that writes those 48. Writing
    44 nearly-identical rules by hand is how one quietly gets the
    wrong sound; and when I found the word-final rule was wrong, one
    edit fixed all 33 consonants at once.

The rules do contain the real Hindi logic:
  virama  क्ष  -> no vowel, it is a cluster
  matra   का   -> the sign's vowel instead of "a"
  word end राम -> the "a" is dropped, so raam and not raama

The new notation caught a real mistake: I wrote "backtrack" where
nothing had been planted, and it refused to compile. In the old
notation that would have compiled and crashed days later somewhere
unrelated.

THE BLOCKER, plainly:
My rules are shaped like Italian's - they take the two ends of the
text as arguments. English's letter rules are shaped differently:
they take one argument and find the text through pre-made wrapper
routines, one per sound. Mine have no wrappers.

Worse: the thing that decides which letter rule to call uses a
75-slot lookup table indexed by character code. Devanagari sits
above slot 75. So even with the right shape, no Devanagari character
can currently reach any rule I wrote.

WHAT IS LEFT

  2. reshape the 48 rules to English's convention - one edit to the
     generator, not 48 edits
  3. extend or replace the thing that picks a letter rule, so
     Devanagari characters reach ours

Steps 1 and 2 are small. Step 3 is the unknown: if there is a clean
hook, quick. If the whole 600-line dispatcher has to be rewritten,
several sessions.

Estimate to hear recognisable Hindi words: 2-4 more sessions. Not one.

"Perfect Hindi" is not a promise I can make. 18 of the 59 letters
still point at approximate sounds - the breathy ones (भ ध घ) and the
tongue-curled ones (ट ड ण) do not exist as sounds in the engine yet,
and each new one has to be judged by ear, because there is no IBM

aim at. Perfect has no finish line I can see from here.

One note for later: the two detours above are both caused by Hindi
sitting on English. Italian's convention is the one the Polish work
proved, and my rules were already shaped that way. The Devanagari

whichever base is used.
